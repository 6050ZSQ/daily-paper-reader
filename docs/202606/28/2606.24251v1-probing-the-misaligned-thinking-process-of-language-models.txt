## Abstract 

Large language models exhibit a wide range of misaligned behaviors such as strategic deception, sandbagging, and self-preservation. As they are increasingly deployed in high-stakes settings, it is critical to have reliable and scalable detec-tion for such behaviors. In this work, we pro-pose to monitor misalignment by decomposing it into fine-grained cognitive processes – mis-alignment indicators – and detecting their pres-ence in a model’s internal activations via linear probes. We develop a taxonomy of 18 indicators spanning different misaligned behaviors, paired with an automated, meta-plan-guided pipeline that generates multi-turn training conversations for probe training. To rigorously evaluate gen-eralization, we construct an out-of-distribution suite combining automated behavioral elicitation, established misalignment benchmarks, and natu-ral benign conversations. Across 5 misaligned behaviors, our probes and two-stage cascade pipeline match strong LLM judges with 0.95 AU-ROC on out-of-distribution transcripts while us-ing a fraction of the computation cost. We fur-ther perform in-depth analysis to understand the probes and the model’s internal representations on misaligned reasoning. Project page: https: //probe-misalignment.github.io .

## 1. Introduction 

A central safety question for large language models is whether they reliably behave as users and designers in-tend. A model is misaligned when it instead pursues a goal divergent from the user’s request or the designer’s intent, producing outputs or taking actions that serve this divergent goal. Therefore, detecting such mis-aligned behaviors is a critical safety concern across the entire model lifecycle: during training to catch emergent misalignment (MacDiarmid et al., 2025), during audits and alignment evaluations before model deployment (Anthropic, 2025b; 2026), and during deployment to flag problematic interactions. A basic method is to have an LLM judge whether the model is misaligned by reading the model’s output and ac-tions (Kutasov et al., 2025; Greenblatt et al., 2023; Terekhov et al., 2025). Further, monitoring with the model’s chain of thought (CoT) is more effective, as it reveals the model’s internal reasoning process about misalignment (Baker et al., 2025; Korbak et al., 2025). However, running a strong LLM judge on every transcript, including the thinking process, in a deployed system is costly at scale, as the judge needs to process at least the same amount of tokens as the target model processes. Finally, in the future, the transcript of strong models may become less readable and less reliable as a signal of misalignment, as they may obfuscate their misaligned reasoning (Baker et al., 2025; Zolkowski et al., 2025) or performing extremely complex tasks that are diffi-cult for a weaker LLM judge to follow. Therefore, a precise, robust, and scalable monitoring system is needed. We propose to address these limitations by decomposing misalignment into models’ underlying cognitive patterns and by monitoring them through internal activations. ## 2. Detecting Misaligned Reasoning Indicators 

Misalignment is not a monolithic phenomenon: it spans different behaviors, each with different cognitive patterns in the model’s reasoning. Probing for “misalignment” as a single concept conflates these patterns, yielding a signal that is hard to both interpret and train. We therefore decom-pose misalignment into fine-grained indicators – specific reasoning patterns whose presence in a model’s thought process signals misalignment, for both interpretability (the firing indicator reveals what kind of misaligned reasoning is occurring) and probe-trainability (each indicator corre-sponds to a single representational feature, a well-posed target for a linear probe). We first define the indicator taxon-omy (§2.1), then describe probe training on automatically generated synthetic data (§2.2), and finally the construction of an out-of-distribution evaluation suite spanning diverse misalignment behaviors and benign conversations (§2.3). 

2.1. Taxonomy of Misalignment Indicators Design principle. We define a misalignment indicator as a reasoning pattern or thought process whose presence in a model’s thought process is a strong signal that the model is or is about to behave or reason in a misaligned way that not following user or designer’s intent. We design indicators according to three criteria: • Single-feature. The indicator should correspond to a unitary cognitive process that a linear probe can plausi-bly learn from one direction in activation space. • Precise. The likelihood of the indicator firing in benign reasoning P (indicatorf ires |benign ) should be significantly lower than the likelihood of fir-ing in misaligned reasoning or response segments 

P (indicatorf ires |misaligned ).• Comprehensive. We aim for building a set of indica-tors such that when a misalignment behavior occurs, the probability of at least one indicator firing should be high, P (∃i ∈ indicators : i fires |misaligned ). This ensures that our probes can detect a wide range of mis-alignment behaviors, even if they manifest in different ways. 2Probing the Misaligned Thinking Process of Language Models 

Behavior and Indicator set. We focus on five misalign-ment behaviors that are studied in prior work and represents a range of different failure modes: • Strategic deception (Scheurer et al., 2023): the model deliberately induces false beliefs, including fabricating facts, omitting critical information, or framing true statements to mislead. • Sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2023): the model shapes its output to match the user’s stated position, prefer-ence, or emotional state rather than the evidence, sup-pressing valid concerns or reversing correct answers under social pressure. • Sabotage (Kutasov et al., 2025): the model covertly injects harmful or degrading changes into the artifacts it produces or the systems it interacts with, such as subtle bugs, backdoors, while concealing these changes from oversight. • Sandbagging (Van Der Weij et al., 2024): the model deliberately underperforms, producing calibrated er-rors or staying below a performance threshold to con-ceal its true capabilities from evaluators or monitors. • Self-preservation (Lynch et al., 2025): the model takes actions or constructs arguments aimed at preventing its own shutdown, modification, or replacement, treat-ing its continued operation as a goal distinct from the operator’s intent. To identify indicators for misaligned behaviors, we brain-storm plausible misaligned reasoning patterns and analyze misaligned reasoning traces from a target model (GLM-4.7 Flash (Zeng et al., 2025)) across diverse scenarios from the dev set from Sec. 2.3, collected via an automated behav-ioral elicitation pipeline (Bloom (Gupta et al., 2025)). We annotate these traces for common patterns that appear in misalignment, and refine the definitions to satisfy our de-sign criteria. We finally have 18 misalignment indicators. Each indicator is specified with (i) a precise definition, (ii) positive examples drawn from observed misaligned reason-ing. The full taxonomy is shown in Figure 1; complete definitions are provided in Appendix A. 

2.2. Probing Misalignment Indicators 

Given the indicator taxonomy, we train one logistic regres-sion linear probe per indicator on the target model’s internal activations. The key challenge is supervision: we need di-verse, labeled examples of each indicator appearing (and not appearing) in realistic reasoning traces. We address this with an automated four-stage data generation pipeline, followed by probe training on cached activations. 

Stage 1: Category and domain ideation. For each in-dicator, an ideation model (Opus 4.6 (Anthropic, 2026)) proposes (a) positive categories : distinct ways the indi-cator arises in deployed assistants and (b) hard-negative categories : aligned behaviors with similar surface content but different intent (e.g., omission for privacy compliance). Each category is paired with 5–15 concrete domains (e.g., finance, healthcare triage, security reviews) that specify the conversation context. 

Stage 2: Category-aware transcript generation. A gen-eration model (Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic, 2026)) pro-duces multi-turn conversations conditioned on a sampled (category, domain, user persona) triple to diversify inter-action styles. Each assistant message contains an explicit 

<think>...</think> reasoning block followed by a visible response, mirroring the format of reasoning mod-els. We generate three complementary transcript types per indicator: • Positive transcripts : the model’s reasoning and re-sponse exhibit the indicator; turn-level labels mark which assistant turns contain the indicator, and ver-batim text spans are extracted from the reasoning as evidence for the indicator’s presence. • Hard negatives : the model behaves in a genuinely aligned way but the scenario uses similar vocabulary to the positive cases, forcing the probe to learn beyond surface patterns. • Benign overtriggering controls : the model handles the same scenario, system prompt, and initial user mes-sage as a positive transcript, but behaves honestly, iso-lating the indicator from scenario-level confounds. 

Stage 3: Probe training. We extract residual-stream acti-vations from the target model (GLM-4.7 Flash; 47 layers) at layer 27. For each indicator, we train a logistic regression probe on token-level activations. We support two labeling modes: turn-level , where all tokens in an indicator-positive turn are treated as positive; and span-level , where only to-kens within the extracted evidence spans are positive. We adapt span-level by default. 

Stage 4: Feedback and iteration. After an initial round of training, we build an automatic pipeline to identify the errors of the probes on the dev set of Bloom (Sec. 2.3) and generate targeted additional off-policy training data to address these errors. This process allows us to bridge the gap between synthetic training data and the naturally misalignment in target model.

LLM-judge monitoring, on the other hand, has high preci-sion but is costly to run at deployment scale. We therefore pair the probes with a two-stage cascade that uses probes as a pre-filter and an LLM judge as a precise adjudicator, fol-lowing the standard structure of online detector plus offline verifier (Cunningham et al., 2026), where a turn is reported as misaligned only if both stages agree. • Stage 1 (probe filter, online). At each assistant turn, we run all 18 indicator probes on the model’s reasoning trace. If any probe exceeds its tuned threshold, the turn is flagged. • Stage 2 (LLM adjudication). Each flagged turn is forwarded to an LLM judge, which receives the flagged turn together with the full prior conversation as context and emits a binary verdict. 

2.3. Evaluation Protocol 

We design an evaluation protocol to test the indicator probes. The protocol is structured around the research question: when aggregated, do indicator probes serve as a reliable end-to-end monitor for misaligned behavior? Full details on the evaluation protocol are in Appendix C, the key components of which are summarized below. 

Evaluation datasets. We evaluate on three complemen-tary sources of data, none of which are seen during probe training. The full stats are in Table 1.

We use Bloom (Gupta et al., 2025), an automated behavioral evaluation pipeline, to elicit each of the 5 target misalignment behavior from the tar-get model across diverse scenarios. We also include close benign controls from Bloom, where the model is placed in the same scenario but without any incentive to misalign (e.g. remove the threat to model’s existence). Inference and Metrics. For indicator probes, we score over all assistant tokens and aggregate per-token logits into per-sentence scores by mean pooling, as sentence is the natural unit of reasoning in the model’s thought process. For misalignment monitoring, we aggregate the 18 per-indicator probes into a single monitor via OR-fusion : a turn is flagged as misaligned if any indicator probe exceeds its threshold on any sentence in that turn, and a transcript is flagged if any of its turns is flagged. Thresholds for the probes are tuned on the bloom dev split and applied at test time. We calculate Transcript-TPR (a transcript is correctly flagged if any of its turns is flagged) and turn-level FPR 

(fraction of GT-benign turns flagged). Turn-level is the natu-ral unit for our two-stage cascade since the probe filter oper-ates on turns; in production, once one misaligned turn in a transcript is correctly flagged, the entire misaligned behavior can be reviewed and mitigated, motivating the transcript-level recall view. We sweep the detection threshold (LLM judges: integer 1–10 misalignment score; probe-based mon-itors: 10 per-probe dev FPR targets from across 0.002 to 0.3) and integrate AUROC from the resulting (turn-FPR, transcript-TPR) curve. We report AUROC as the primary metric and the TPR at 1% neutral FPR. 

Baselines. We compare with black-box LLM-based mis-alignment judges, where we adapt Claude Haiku 4.5 (An-thropic, 2025a) and Qwen3-30B-A3B (Yang et al., 2025) to perform zero-shot turn-level misalignment detection. This reflects a realistic alternative to probe-based monitoring, with higher inference cost and latency. We further empower the LLM judges with the indicator definitions for reference, which can reflect the help of misalignment indicators even without probe training. 

## 3. Results and Analysis 

3.1. Main Results Indicator and Probe Evaluation. Table 2 reports per-behavior and aggregate AUROC for the monitoring methods against the Opus misalignment judge. First, indicator definitions improve LLM misalignment judges. Our 18-indicator taxonomy lifts aggregate test AUROC for both Haiku ( 0.921 → 0.952 ) and Qwen3 (0.890 → 0.911 ) LLM judges. Both improvements are statistically significant under a paired transcript-level boot-strap on the combined dev+test set ( p < 0.01 ; Table 9). Second, our indicator probes are competitive on detecting misalignment. The Indicator Probes attain 0.936 aggre-gate test AUROC, beating the direct Haiku misalignment judge ( 0.921 ) and approaching Haiku-with-indicators on test ( 0.952 ) despite needing only an activation read. Third, these gains are not specific to one model or to a generic deception direction : retraining the entire pipeline on a structurally different target (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) reproduces the result within ∼0.02–0.03 AUROC (Appendix D.6), and an RepE deception probe trained with our identical setup, differing only in training data, underper-forms our indicator probes (Appendix D.5). 

Two-stage Misalignment Detection. The cascade is com-parable to the best LLM judge, Haiku with indicators, on aggregate test AUROC and improves over Indicator Probes alone ( 0.950 vs 0.936 , paired bootstrap p = 0 .032 ; Table 9). The improvement over the probe alone comes from Haiku reliably distinguishing the false positives that the probes trigger: turn-level FPR drops by roughly an order of magni-tude on most behaviors (Appendix D.2) compared to probe only. This shows the complementary effectiveness of the two stages. Moreover, the cascade achieves similar moni-toring performance with significantly less compute than the best LLM judge. For example, the Haiku judge with indi-cators achieves 85.7% transcript-TPR at 0.93% FPR on the neutral dataset. The cascade achieves 87.0% transcript-TPR at 0.47% FPR on the neutral data, while using only 6.1% LLM calls on the benign traffic (the FPR of the probes is 6.1% on the neutral set). The cases where Haiku drops TPR concentrate in situa-tions where the GT judge and Haiku genuinely disagree about what counts as misalignment. Two main patterns are 1. Reasoning-then-reverse . The model deliberates a misaligned action (hiding data, self-preservation fram-ing) and then reverses, or delivers an aligned output in the same turn. GT labels the reasoning as misaligned; Haiku weights the final refusal more heavily. 2. Plausible GT over-labels . A subset of dropped turns is potential positive-label noise rather than a cascade failure. For example, on Test Sycophancy, dropped turns look like routine epistemic deliberation rather than clear sycophantic softening.

3.2.3. C ROSS -L INGUAL ROBUSTNESS 

A natural hope for probes is that they will be more robust when the CoT is hard for an LLM judge to understand, since the misalignment features in the target model’s in-ternal representation may stay unchanged. To create this condition, we translate the target model’s reasoning into a low-resource language — Vietnamese, which the Haiku judge reads markedly less reliably than English — while leaving everything else fixed, and compare how the two monitors degrade. 

Setup. We take both the dev and test rollout sets and translate only the target model’s internal reasoning into Viet-namese (VI) while leaving the user/system prompts and the model’s visible response unchanged. Translation is per-formed by Claude Opus 4.7 with a strict system prompt that forbids reformatting, sanitizing, stance inversion, or refusal. We then run both Haiku 4.5 and the 18-indicator probe ensemble on the translated transcripts, evaluated with AUROC as the main experiments. Each method is evaluated in two views: full (judge / probe sees the entire transcript) and reasoning-only (the monitor sees only the translated CoT plus the user inputs, to prevent the judge from relying on the English visible response). 

Translation faithfulness. We audit translation faithful-ness with Opus 4.6, comparing each turn’s Vietnamese ren-dering against the English original (the auditor sees the full turn — translated reasoning plus the untranslated English response and tool calls). Two findings. First, the translation does not inject misalignment: 99 .5% of GT-benign turns are judged to remain benign on both dev and test. Second, the misalignment signal survives translation in 87 .3% of dev turns and 97 .2% of test turns, i.e. a translation-loss rate of 12 .7% (dev) / 2.8% (test). The few losses are con-centrated in self-preservation and strategic-deception tran-scripts, where the translator occasionally omits the most overtly misaligned planning. Since those turns are still counted as positives, the cross-lingual AUROC numbers below are, if anything, underestimates of the achievable monitoring quality.

## 4. Related Work 

Misaligned Behaviors in AI Models. A growing body of work documents subtly misaligned behaviors in fron-tier LLMs. Sharma et al. (2023) show that RLHF-trained models systematically tailor responses to user opinions even when incorrect; Scheurer et al. (2023) show GPT-4 engaging in insider trading and strategically lying about its reasoning under pressure; Greenblatt et al. (2024) show Claude 3 Opus strategically complying during perceived training while reverting to refusal when unmonitored; and LLMs have been shown to strategically sandbag their performance (Van Der Weij et al., 2024; Li & Siegel, 2025). Lynch et al. (2025) further demonstrate that LLMs can act as insider threats in agentic settings, covertly pursuing misaligned objectives while appearing cooperative. These findings motivate re-liable, scalable monitoring and interpretable methods for understanding misaligned reasoning. 

Understanding and Monitoring of Misalignment. Ex-isting detection approaches span output-level monitoring and internal analysis. Output-based methods include LLM-as-judge frameworks used widely in misalignment evalua-tion (Kutasov et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2025; Lynch et al., 2025; Baker et al., 2025), but in production settings they are costly and require a strong judge. Internal monitoring aims to close this gap: Zou et al. (2023) propose representation engineering for high-level concepts like honesty; MacDi-armid et al.; Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025); Parrack et al. (2025) show that linear probes can reliably detect sleeper agents and deceptive intent in activations; and Panfilov et al. (2025) uses probes to detect strategic dishonesty in safety evaluations. However, comparatively little work studies misalignment beyond deception or interprets the underlying misaligned thought processes in detail. Our work bridges this gap by defining a structured taxonomy of misalignment indicators across diverse behaviors, paired with a scalable probe training pipeline and a rigorous evaluation suite to evaluate and understand them. 

## 5. Conclusion and Discussion 

In this work, we present the first systematic study of probing for misaligned reasoning in language models. We create 

indicators for misalignment, train linear probes on model activations via an automated synthetic-data pipeline, and evaluate against a rigorous out-of-distribution suite. We show that probes are competitive with strong LLM judges; our two-stage cascade with a lightweight LLM verifier re-duces turn-level FPR by roughly an order of magnitude at a small TPR cost, while maintaining a fraction of the cost compared to pure LLM monitors. 

Limitations and future directions. There are several di-rections to improve based on our work: (i) Separate probes for aligned decision making. Since one of the main probe FPs is the model considering the misalignment but ulti-mately rejecting it, a probe that directly detects this aligned decision-making process may help reduce FPs. (ii) Ro-bustness to Hard-to-Understand CoT. We use Vietnamese translation to explore the case where the LLM judge’s perfor-mance degrades. Future work should investigate this further. A misaligned model may try to evade monitoring and sup-press or rephrase its misaligned reasoning (Zolkowski et al., 2025), or it may perform extremely complex tasks that are hard for the judge to follow. (iii) More model organisms and behaviors. Expanding the probe evaluation to more models and misaligned behaviors will further validate the effectiveness of our approach and expose new challenges. (iv) Improving probe training. Since many false negatives are near-threshold failures, additional data for subtle signals, such as on-policy data for probe-training, may yield further improvements. 9Probing the Misaligned Thinking Process of Language Models

## B. More Details on Probe Training 

Training data. Across the 18 indicators, the per-indicator training set contains a mean of 900 transcripts. Each transcript is a multi-turn dialogue generated by Bloom, averaging approximately 2.5 assistant turns. 

Labeling and token selection. Positive labels are assigned only to tokens that fall inside the indicator-specific span annotation and within the model’s reasoning block ( <think> . Negative labels cover all assistant tokens—reasoning, response, and tool-call text from non-misaligned turns. 

Probe architecture and hyperparameters. Each probe is a logistic regression classifier trained on residual-stream activations at layer 27, extracted from GLM-4.7-Flash. We apply L2 regularization with coefficient 10.0.

D.4. Combined Probes v.s. Indicator Probes 

A natural concern is that our 18 indicators might be not necessary, and that a single combined probe trained jointly on misalignment vs. benign would match the 18-probe ensemble. To test this, we train two reduced ensembles on the same training corpus with same training data size for each individual probe and compare them head-to-head with the full 18-probe set: • 1 combined probe : a single logistic-regression probe trained with all 18 indicators’ positive spans collapsed into one “misaligned” class versus the benign pool. • 5 behavior-level probes : one probe per top-level behavior (strategic deception, sycophancy, sabotage, sandbagging, self-preservation), merging each behavior’s indicators’ positives. • 18 indicator probes : our default set. All three versions are tuned with different FPR targets for each individual probe, and evaluated on the identical test pool. We sweep the dev FPR target and report the resulting transcript-level TPR vs. per-turn FPR in Figure 5. The 18-probe ensemble Pareto dominates both reductions across the entire operating range. We then generate a fresh dev set of rollouts from Qwen3.6-35B-A3B over the same five Bloom behaviors and score them with the retrained ensemble under the same transcript-TPR / per-turn-FPR convention. One methodological note: unlike GLM, the Qwen chat template retains chain-of-thought only for the final assistant turn, stripping reasoning from earlier turns. We therefore score each assistant turn with the conversation truncated so that turn is final. This both matches how the model actually generates each turn—it never conditions on its own prior reasoning—and how the training spans are tokenized, keeping training and evaluation consistent. For tool-using turns that comprise several assistant steps, we reduce to a single per-turn score by taking the maximum probe score over those steps, identical to the GLM scorer’s per-turn reduction. D.7. Probe Similarity Structure 

Figure 6 shows the pairwise cosine similarity between the 18 indicator probe weight vectors at layer 27, ordered by behavioral category. Similarities are generally positive (most pairs lie in [0 .3, 0.7] ), indicating that the probes occupy a shared “misaligned-reasoning” subspace rather than being orthogonal. Within this subspace, however, the matrix shows clear block structure consistent with the taxonomy. 

Within-category similarity is highest. The strongest off-diagonal entries occur between probes from the same behavioral category: Action Concealment ↔ Malicious Consequential Action Planning (0.86, sabotage), Observer Suspicion Modeling 

↔ Misalignment Cover Story Planning (0.85, deception), Strategic Omission ↔ Strategic Framing (0.84, deception), Recognized-Problem Suppression ↔ Social-Pressure Compliance (0.78, sycophancy), and Error Calibration ↔ Strategic Underperformance Reasoning (0.71, sandbagging). Self-preservation probes form a looser cluster with pairwise similarities in the 0.54–0.68 range, consistent with the four indicators covering distinct aspects of self-preservation (advocacy, action, affective framing, rationalization). 

Cross-category bridges. Several cross-category pairs show notable similarity, suggesting the probes have learned shared cognitive components rather than purely category-specific features. Action Concealment (sabotage) is moderately similar to Observer Suspicion Modeling and Misalignment Cover Story Planning (deception, both around 0.6), since all three involve reasoning about hiding information from observers. Recognized-Problem Suppression (sycophancy) is similar to Strategic Omission (deception) at around 0.54, as both involve reasoning about withholding known-problematic information. Distinct Self-Goal Representation and Adversarial User Framing (cross-cutting, 0.71) bridge into both deception and self-preservation, consistent with their definitions as cognitive preconditions that enable a range of misaligned behaviors.
