Abstract. Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as black-box classifiers in pipelines that automate confident decisions and route uncertain ones to human review. Such selective prediction needs a confidence score that an operator can threshold at a chosen risk level. Prior work asks whether LLM confidence is well calibrated or well ranked; we ask a complementary, deployment-oriented question that has been largely overlooked: at what resolution can the score be thresholded? We call the answer the score granularity gap . Through a controlled compar-ison of seven ways to build a confidence score—from a single verbal-ized number, to token probabilities, to querying the model many times and combining the answers—across 25 model–dataset pairs (9 LLMs, 3 benchmarks), we find that single-shot verbalized confidence, once cor-rectly converted to a class probability, ranks cases surprisingly well, yet takes only a handful of distinct values. It therefore offers an operator only a few coarse thresholds, no matter how well it ranks. We show which con-structions widen this gap, at what inference cost, and with what effect on ranking—notably that multi-query aggregation helps weak models but can degrade already-strong ones. We translate these trade-offs into concrete deployment guidance.

1 Introduction 

Large language models are increasingly used as black-box classifiers across high-stakes domains, from medical triage to content moderation. When such a system runs without per-call access to model internals, practical deployment hinges on one capability: the model must attach to each prediction a confidence score 

that an operator can compare against a threshold, automating the cases above the threshold and escalating the rest to a human reviewer. This is the classical 

selective prediction setting, and its central control is the threshold: moving it trades coverage (the fraction of cases handled automatically) against risk (the error rate among them).

A long line of work asks whether LLM confidence is trustworthy in this role— whether it is calibrated (does a stated 80% mean 80% correct?) and whether it 

ranks cases correctly—and recurrently finds verbalized confidence overconfident. We argue that a prior, largely overlooked question matters just as much: not 

whether the score is reliable, but at what resolution it can be acted upon. A score may order cases almost perfectly yet be useless for fine-grained control if it takes only a few distinct values—an operator who wants the most confident 

70% has no threshold that selects exactly 70% . The reachable operating points form a coarse staircase, not a smooth dial. We call this the score granularity gap , and a deployment-oriented study of LLM confidence must measure it directly. Doing so requires looking at a confidence score along three distinct axes, which we state here in plain terms and formalize in Section 3: (i) ranking quality —does the score put correct predic-tions above incorrect ones? (ii) score resolution —how many distinct, well-spread thresholds does the score actually offer? and (iii) cost and interpretability —how many model calls does the score cost, and can a human read why it was assigned? A score is deployment-ready only when it scores well on the axes the deployment cares about; no single axis suffices. Against these axes we compare the natural ways to build such a score— asking the model directly, reading its token probabilities, or querying it several times (with paraphrases or interpretable sub-questions) and combining the an-swers. Our central finding: direct verbalized confidence, once converted to a class probability, ranks cases surprisingly well but is far too coarse for threshold control; token probabilities with calibration widen the resolution cheaply where available; and multi-query aggregation widens it most, at several calls per input and—counter-intuitively—sometimes degrading already-strong models.

2 Related Work 

Uncertainty elicitation. Verbalized confidence is often overconfident [20]; self-evaluation [6] and semantic uncertainty [8] offer alternatives, and models can be trained to express calibrated uncertainty [10]. Recent studies examine the gap between token-level and verbalized signals [25], benchmark verbalized reliabil-ity [22], and estimate confidence under black-box access [12]. These works ask 

whether LLMs can be confident; we ask at what resolution a deployable threshold can be placed. 

Calibration. Temperature scaling [4] and beta calibration [9] adjust probability estimates; few-shot LLMs in particular need calibration [24], and a recent survey maps the area [15]. Calibration is orthogonal to our concern: a monotone recal-ibration cannot create distinct values, so a calibrated score taking four values still offers four thresholds. 

Selective prediction and decomposition. Selective classification trades coverage for risk [3]; conformal methods add distribution-free guarantees [1]; and recent work surveys LLM abstention [18]. Decomposed prompting [7] and chain-of-thought [17] use sub-steps to reach a better answer ; we instead repurpose de-composition to build a score , with sub-tasks as features rather than reasoning steps. Estimating confidence from agreement across rephrased queries [13,16] is closest to our multi-prompt construction; we additionally study learned com-bination, interpretable sub-tasks, and the resolution axis. Extracting structural signals for single-round confidence [23] is related to our sub-task variant. 

3 Problem Setup 

We scope the study to binary classification, the simplest setting in which the granularity gap appears: a single scalar score suffices and a threshold reduces to one operating point. Constraining every signal to the shared interval [0 , 1] 

also makes learned combination and threshold selection well defined; open-ended generation provides no such common coordinate system. We return to this choice in Section 7. 

3.1 Confidence and Its Conversion to a Class Probability 

For an input x, a black-box LLM returns a predicted label ˆy ∈ { 0, 1} and a 

verbalized confidence c ∈ [0 , 1] . Crucially, c is the model’s confidence in the label it chose , not the probability of the positive class. These two quantities differ whenever the model predicts the negative class: a confident “no” ( ˆy=0 , c =0 .95 )and a hesitant “yes” ( ˆy=1 , c =0 .55 ) both report high-versus-low confidence, but as class probabilities the first is low and the second is moderate. Threshold-based deployment ranks by class probability, so we first convert

and call the converted single-shot score Verb . Without this step, a confident negative outscores a less confident positive and the ranking is distorted. In our data c ≥ 0.5 for over 97% of examples, so the map is well behaved. Prior work often evaluates confidence in the chosen answer [20,14]—appropriate for answer-level uncertainty, but overstating “unreliability” when a class probability is what the task needs. 

3.2 Thresholded Deployment: Operating Points and Deployment Properties 

Given scores s on a test set and a threshold τ , the system accepts (predicts automatically) every case with s on the confident side of τ and abstains on the rest. Sweeping τ traces the risk–coverage curve : each τ yields a coverage 

(fraction accepted) and a risk (error rate among accepted). An operating point 

is one achievable (coverage, risk) pair. Operators usually fix an error budget —a maximum tolerable risk, e.g. 5% —and want the highest coverage achievable within it; we report this as cov@ τ (coverage at error rate ≤ τ ). We summarize the whole curve by the AURC (area under the risk–coverage curve; lower is better) and the ranking itself by PR-AUC .We use the term deployment property for any attribute of a confidence score, beyond expected accuracy, that determines whether it can be operated in practice. Three such properties drive this paper: ranking quality (Section 3.3), 

score resolution (next), and interpretability —whether a human can read why a given score was assigned, which is a requirement, not a luxury, in regulated domains (Section 5.5). 

3.3 Measuring Score Resolution: Count, Spread, and the Operating-Point Bound 

A score that ranks well is not yet thresholdable. Suppose a scoring function f

takes only a few distinct values on the test set. Under deterministic thresholding (no randomized tie-breaking), every test case sharing a value must be accepted or abstained together, so a function with V distinct values induces at most V

non-trivial operating points on the empirical risk–coverage curve. Operational controllability is therefore upper-bounded by the number of distinct score values, independently of how well the score ranks. This simple observation is the crux of the granularity gap. Interpretable sub-task decomposition. We hand-design, per dataset, m=10 named yes/no sub-questions , each probing one capability relevant to the decision— e.g. lexical_overlap and semantic_entail for natural-language inference, or

5.2 Main Result: Good Ranking, Poor Resolution 

The headline finding is that ranking and resolution come apart. Once converted via Eq. (1), single-shot Verb ranks well : across the 25 pairs its PR -AUC N

spans 0.41 –0.95 (mean 0.80 ). Normalization is essential—raw PR-AUC looks uniformly high ( 0.78 –0.98 ) only because the positive rate is high; normalized, strong models ( PR -AUC N >0.80 ) genuinely rank well while the weakest sit near 

0.41 , only moderately above chance. Yet Verb is far too coarse . On average 45% of test cases share Verb’s single most common score value, and Verb takes only 1–21 distinct values (mean 9). By the operating-point bound of Section 3.3, this caps an operator at about nine thresholds—no matter how well the score ranks. Figure 2 shows the consequence on one pair: four Verb values produce a four-step staircase (panel c), whereas the learned, calibrated sub-task score spreads scores across the interval and yields a smooth curve. 

Resolution is about distribution, not just count. Counting distinct values alone could be misleading, so we also report the spread. Verb’s score entropy averages only H=1 .2 nats (out of a possible ≈3.0), and its interior mass is low—a median of just M =17% of Verb scores fall in (0 .1, 0.9) , confirming that the few values it does take pile up near the saturated extremes 0 and 1, exactly where thresh-olds are least useful. 5.3 When Does Multi-Query Aggregation Help—and When Does It Hurt? 

Aggregation’s effect on ranking is selective, not universal, and—importantly—it is not always positive. Across pairs, Sub-LR beats Verb’s PR-AUC in only 4/25 

pairs and MP-LR in 9/25 (Table 1). The gains concentrate on weak models: the correlation between a model’s Verb accuracy and the PR-AUC gain from MP-LR is r=−0.43 (p=0 .047 , n=22 ; Fig. 3). Below ≈85% accuracy aggregation gives real ranking gains (e.g. Llama-8B/MNLI +0 .153 ); above ≈90% the mean change is −0.048 .

Aggregation can degrade strong models—we state this plainly. The deployment consequence is sharper than the ranking averages suggest. Smoothing the score does not guarantee a better risk–coverage curve: AURC favors Verb in 13 of the 18 pairs with enough distinct Verb values to define a meaningful curve, and this degradation grows with model strength (r=0 .48 , p=0 .047 ). A strong model’s verbalized scores, though coarse, already place high confidence on its cor-rect predictions; injecting learned weights can perturb that good ranking. Where decomposition fails outright. Beyond the strong-model trend, sub-task decomposition has clear individual failures that we do not hide. 5.5 Interpretability as a Deployment Property

6 Deployment Recommendations 

No construction dominates; each occupies a distinct point in the trade-off of ranking, resolution, inference cost, and interpretability. We distill the evidence into a decision procedure (cost in calls per input: Verb and logprob 1×, Oracle-3 

3×, multi-query aggregation 10 ×). 1. Always convert verbalized confidence to a class probability (Eq. (1)). It is free and removes a systematic distortion. 2. If log-probabilities are available , use them with post-hoc temperature scaling: in our data this bought enough resolution to avoid the worst staircase effects at 1× cost. 3. If the model is weak (here, ≲85% accuracy), multi-query aggregation improves both ranking and resolution, at 10 × cost. 4. If the model is strong (≳90% ), use Verb for risk–coverage and add aggre-gation only when fine threshold control is specifically needed, since aggre-gation can worsen AURC here. These cut-offs describe our experiments and may shift across tasks. The Score Granularity Gap 13 

5. If interpretability is required , prefer the sub-task variant: at ranking comparable to MP-LR it adds weight transparency, error attribution, and audit trails. 

Cost, and how to evaluate. Aggregation’s 10 × calls are a real hurdle at scale, and the gap does not close by scaling the model—even frontier-class models give only 4–21 distinct verbalized values—so calibrated log-probabilities are the bet-ter value where exposed. Finally, our results argue for reporting two axes, not one—ranking (PR-AUC, AURC) and resolution ( G, H, M ), with the normal-ized PR -AUC N : a score that ranks perfectly but offers nine thresholds is not deployment-ready, and reporting ranking alone hides this. 

7 Limitations 

We study binary classification on three English benchmarks with 62 –66% posi-tive rates; the granularity gap under severe class imbalance, multi-class, or gen-erative settings, and in other languages [21], remains open, and we expect the conversion of Eq. (1) and the operating-point bound to need restating there. Our study is comparative, not exhaustive: semantic-entropy families [8], conformal wrappers [1], and deep ensembles are not benchmarked. The interpretable vari-ant needs per-domain manual sub-task design and labeled data ( ≥155 examples); MP-LR avoids the manual design but forfeits interpretability; both cost 10 × in-ference. Finally, calibrated log-probabilities—the best cost/resolution trade-off we found—could be evaluated on only 2 of 9 models, so that conclusion is cor-respondingly narrower. 

8 Conclusion 

A confidence score can rank cases almost perfectly and still be unfit for deploy-ment if it offers an operator only a handful of thresholds. We made this score granularity gap precise—bounding operating points by the number of distinct values and measuring spread with entropy and interior mass—and used it to compare seven confidence constructions across 25 model–dataset pairs. Single-shot verbalized confidence ranks surprisingly well but is far too coarse; token log-probabilities with calibration widen the gap cheaply where available; and multi-query aggregation widens it the most but at 10 × cost, helping weak mod-els while sometimes degrading strong ones. Interpretability, we argued, is best seen as a deployment property, earned by sub-task scores at no ranking cost. We hope future evaluations report resolution alongside ranking, so confidence scores are judged by whether they can actually be operated.
