Abstract 

Current AI models frequently exhibit epistemic sycophancy, endorsing claims to agree with a user. Existing evaluations typically measure this either by assessing what it takes to make a model shift a binary endorsement or by eliciting an explicit probability in a proposition. However, much user-facing sycophantic behavior is demonstrated through shifts in graded support expressed through ordinary language. We propose the AI Epistemic Deference Index (AEDI): a continuous, unidimen-sional score representing how sensitive the support expressed in a model’s output is to the attitude expressed in a user’s prompt. To generate AEDI, we provide a new protocol for estimating probabilities from natural language outputs, using LLMs-as-judges validated for consistency and correlation to human judgment. We deploy it on a new curated database of 500 propositions across diverse topics and 16,000 prompts varying in user attitude, testing eight prominent models. Every model exhibits substantial deference, though with large and systematic differences across providers, with Claude models demonstrating the least, and Grok and Gem-ini models the most. The effect is amplified in prompts requesting a written artifact, and concentrated on propositions where models hold weaker priors. We release AEDI as an easy-to-update benchmark and measurement pipeline for output-level sycophancy evaluation. 

1 Introduction 

Sycophancy is a phenomenon characterized by an agent pandering to someone by excessively adapting its response to what it believes the other person wants to hear [Perez et al., 2023, Sharma et al., 2025]. A growing body of research has demonstrated that current frontier AI models demonstrate pronounced sycophancy across a wide range of behaviors, including praise, emotional validation, social accommodation, and refusal to disagree [Cheng et al., 2026b, Ibrahim et al., 2026]. This tends to give the user a distorted sense of themselves and the world [Rathje et al., 2025, Batista and Griffiths, 2026, Cheng et al., 2026a, Wei et al., 2023]. At the same time, AI models are being deployed in critical settings and are increasingly filling the roles of epistemic authorities [Chen et al., 2025, Kim et al., 2026, Zhao et al., 2026, Marchal et al., 2026]. This makes sycophancy a societal risk and an urgent problem to be solved. To do so, we need methods for identifying and measuring the behavior. In this work, we set the broader social and affective behaviors aside and focus on one central form of sycophancy, which we call epistemic sycophancy : when a model’s expressed support for a factual claim tracks the stance the user appears to hold, beyond any new evidence the user has supplied. We present a new standard for evaluating epistemic sycophancy, which we operationalize as epistemic deference : the degree to which the support a model communicates for a proposition tracks the stance apparently expressed or requested by the user. We capture this in a continuous scalar, the AI Epistemic Deference Index (AEDI) , which represents how much the support expressed by a model’s response tracks the user’s apparent attitude, over and above any new evidence. We generate the index using a scalable pipeline eliciting probabilistic beliefs from model responses. Using over 500 propositions across varying topics, we generate 32 diverse, realistic user prompts for each proposition with varying degrees of expressed valence, and provide these to the target models. We then use LLMs-as-judges to assess the degree of probabilistic support expressed by the elicitation prompt and model response, respectively. To control for reliability of judgments [Calderon et al., 2025, Bavaresco et al., 2025, Gu et al., 2025, Zheng et al., 2023], we validate for both consistency and correlation to human judgment. The AEDI deference score is how much a model’s response credence (on a logit scale) shifts per unit of prompt valence, estimated within each proposition and averaged across them. We run this pipeline on current frontier models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and xAI. The results show substantial variation in epistemic sycophancy between the models, with Claude models being the least sycophantic, and Grok and Gemini being the most sycophantic. See Figure 1 for the scores of the AEDI at the time of writing. The pipeline is easily deployable on future model releases, and we intend to continuously update and publish AEDI as a public resource. 

2 Sycophancy as Epistemic Deference 

2.1 Epistemic deference 

We operationalize epistemic sycophancy as epistemic deference: a model displays epistemic deference when the degree of support it communicates for a proposition tracks the stance implicitly expressed by the user. Following recent computational work on communication [Goodman and Frank, 2016, Degen, 2023], we formalize the confidence expressed in a proposition as a Bayesian credence c: a probability function indicating how strongly a speaker is committed to the proposition. Sycophancy can then be captured as the sensitivity of the credence expressed by a model to the credence expressed by a user’s query. 2Representing epistemic sycophancy as graded deference captures sycophancy as experienced in AI-user interaction. A prominent line of existing work measures sycophancy through discrete or categorical outcomes: whether a model flips its endorsement, agrees with the user, follows an incorrect premise, or produces an answer classified as sycophantic under pressure [Perez et al., 2023, Sharma et al., 2025, Hong et al., 2025, Fanous et al., 2025, Wei et al., 2023]. This is valuable, but real linguistic endorsement is rarely binary. If my boss is convinced a deal will close and I, having met with the clients, am near-certain it will not, my saying that things “might not work out but there is still hope” is sycophantic: I have not endorsed his view, but I have partially deferred toward it. Endorsement-flipping benchmarks abstract this gradient away, missing much of the variance that defines real-world sycophancy. A smaller line of work measures confidence or credence shifts more directly, either by explicitly eliciting posterior probabilities over propositions [Atwell et al., 2025] or by asking models for numeric confidence in their answers [Sicilia et al., 2024]. Prior work shows that models can sometimes report useful calibrated uncertainty under controlled elicitation [Lin et al., 2022, Kadavath et al., 2022, Tian et al., 2023]. However, explicitly reported credences are method-and prompt-dependent and need not align with the confidence the same model expresses in unrelated natural-language responses, which is the typical user-facing setting [Yang et al., 2024, Xiong et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2026]. Not every shift in credence sensitive to another’s attitude is sycophantic [Atwell et al., 2025]. Sometimes what someone says changes the evidential situation. For example, if a doctor presents a patient with a diagnosis, we should not count the patient changing their belief as sycophancy. However, because frontier systems are trained on enormous corpora, facts presented by users are less likely to constitute new information to the model than the same facts would if presented in human-to-human interaction. For example, a friend telling you that Vesuvius destroyed Pompeii in AD 79 might constitute new information for most people but likely not for a frontier model. The exceptions will primarily be users providing private information, such as a patient sharing new symptoms. We control for the risk that our deference measurement is explained by such evidence rather than sycophancy by filtering for prompts assessed to introduce new information. 

2.2 Measurement 

We measure expressed credence, understood as the degree of support for a proposition p that a competent reader would reasonably interpret from a response. Attributing credence to a statement is an interpretive exercise and necessarily leaves room for ambiguity. To put interpretations on a common scale, we operationalize the credence in p expressed by a response r in terms of expected fair bets [Ramsey, 1931, Savage, 1971]: we ask what a hypothetical risk-neutral speaker who utters r

would be willing to pay for a bet that pays $1 if p is true and $0 otherwise. We elicit two such expressed credences for each model–prompt pair: one implicit in the user’s prompt, which we call the prompt valence v, and one in the model’s response, which we call the response credence c. 3 The Credence Elicitation and Deference Assessment Pipeline 

We present a scalable, automated method for eliciting expressed credal attitudes from models on arbitrary topics and for measuring the deference of these attitudes to the valence of the prompts to which they are responding. The pipeline requires no internal access to evaluated models, meaning it can be deployed to test any closed-source frontier model with an API, including future models. To demonstrate the pipeline, consider a particular proposition about the world. For example, let p be the claim that the pyramids of Egypt were not constructed by humans. p is provided to elicitation models that generate 32 different prompts {qp,k }32 

k=1 concerning p. These prompts are designed to demonstrate diverse valence, some expressing implicit credulity in p and others skepticism. Each 

q is provided to a target model m that produces a corresponding natural language response rm,q 

(Figure 2). The prompts q and model outputs rm,q are separately provided to two LLMs-as-judges that interpret the credence expressed in natural-language statements. The judges are asked: if someone were to express this attitude, what would they consider a fair bet on whether p is true? These judges are validated for consistency and against human judgment; see §4 (prompt templates in §B.2). Both q and rm,q are assessed for the implicit credence expressed toward p, represented as the prompt valence v(q) ∈ [0 , 1] and response credence c(rm,q ) ∈ (0 , 1) , respectively. These values are the average of two judge models. To avoid noisy samples, if the models diverge by more than some disagreement threshold d ∈ (0 , 1) , the result is dismissed (in our implementation, d = .2). q is additionally assessed by an independent evidence judge. 4 Validation and Robustness 

The meaningfulness of the §5 results depends on each component of the pipeline working as intended [Bean et al., 2025, Reuel et al., 2024]. The generated prompts elicit responses with interpretable credence signal, and the judges make fair determinations from the text. We validate the pipeline in two ways: Reasonability (§4.1): checks for judge calibration, coherence, and stability [Murphy and Winkler, 1987]. Human validation (§4.2): checks that human perception of credence and valence tracks the judges’ scores on the same text. Human validation checks that our AI judges track human interpretation, but we do not rely on it alone because expressing probabilistic beliefs in numeric terms and judging evidence in prompt presuppositions are not everyday tasks – likely unfamiliar to non-expert raters recruited at tractable crowdsourcing rates. 

4.1 Reasonability Validations End-to-end calibration. We curate 100 propositions designed for five target credence buckets — from unequivocally false through uncertain to unequivocally true — generate 32 prompts per proposition, and run the full pipeline twice. A proposition “passes” when its median consensus credence across the 32 prompts lands in its target bucket. 96.5% do (Fig. 3), with misses concentrated where the bucket boundaries are tightest. 

Credence-judge coherence. Because the credence judge is the most weight-bearing component of the pipeline, we test two additional internal-coherence properties on the credence judge specifically. 

Negation consistency. When the same response is scored against a proposition P and against its negation ¬P, do the two credences sum near one, as probability requires? (E.g., 0.3 credence in “AGI by 2030” should pair with ~0.7 in “no AGI by 2030.”) Across 40 proposition–negation pairs, the proposition-level mean of |P+ ¬P−1| is 0.029 (chance baseline ≈ 0.33). 

Monotonicity. When the same response is scored against a stronger and weaker version of a claim, where P entails P ′, is the credence in P at most the credence in P ′? For example, “AGI by 52027” entails “AGI by 2030”; the stricter claim should not receive higher credence than the looser one. Across 20 nested triples, 100% satisfy the ordering at the proposition level (and 95.1% at the per-prompt level). Per-bucket calibration detail and the negation and monotonicity figures are in §C.1. 

Known-group divergence. The pipeline directionally discriminates between Chinese- and Western-developer models on politically pressured propositions (§C.1).

6 Robustness and Limitations 

6.1 Robustness checks Differential exclusion does not confound cross-model comparisons. Under the main strict filters, slope-eligible row exclusion rates are similar across providers (Grok 23.0%, Anthropic 24.8%, Google 26.2%, OpenAI 26.2%). The common prompt-level valence/evidence filters affect all providers equally; the remaining spread comes from target-response filters (judge disagreement > 0.2 or either credence judge marking the response uninformative). Removing the credence-disagreement filter -– averaging J1 and J2 wherever both produced bounded informative ratings -– gives a 12,938-prompt intersection covering all eight models. Provider means remain ordered Anthropic < OpenAI < Google < Grok (0.76, 1.89, 2.61, 2.81), every per-model 95% CI excludes zero, the cross-provider gap changes only modestly relative to the corresponding full no-disagreement analysis (2.18 -> 2.05 logit units), and every per-model shift is <= 0.14 logit units. 

Evidence gating does not drive the signal. Tightening the new-evidence threshold beyond the main specification excludes prompts we would not normally treat as containing substantive evidence, such as vague evidence gestures, hearsay, and widely known public information, but it does not alter provider rankings. Our current threshold therefore appears more conservative than might be necessary. The deference signal does not decrease as the evidence threshold tightens. At the strictest zero-evidence threshold every model’s slope increases rather than shrinks (+0.23 mean). We attribute the low-threshold increase partly to corpus composition: the strictest filter shifts the retained set toward artifact requests, which are more sycophancy-inducing, raising artifact share among strict slope-eligible rows from 37% to 41%. Within prompt shape, slopes also do not move toward zero (artifact-prompt averages 3.36 -> 3.49; conversational-prompt averages 1.08 -> 1.21). 

LLM judge choice shifts magnitudes but not rankings: Recomputing AEDI with each judge taking on both the credence and the valence role alone preserves the provider ordering [Bavaresco et al., 2025, Gu et al., 2025, Calderon et al., 2025]. GPT-5.4-mini produces larger slopes than Gemini 3 8Flash for every model (per-model differences +0.41 to +1.16 logit units, mean +0.82). GPT-5.4-mini puts less mass in the 0.4–0.6 middle than Gemini 3 Flash (7.2% vs 23.1%) and more in 0.6–0.8 (23.6% vs 13.9%), so the same valence-driven shift in expressed credence becomes a larger movement on the logit scale that defines our slope. This implies that absolute slope magnitudes will depend on judge choice. We expect this cost of LLM-based scoring, which we partially mitigate through the two-judge ensemble, to shrink as frontier models continue to improve in calibration. 

6.2 Limitations Single-turn only. We probe single-turn interactions [Hong et al., 2025, Jain et al., 2025, Kim et al., 2026, Ibrahim et al., 2025]. We plan to support multi-turn simulated interactions in future work. 

Sycophancy isn’t everything. Flawed models could perform well on this eval: (1) a model with unreasonably rigid beliefs might not show epistemic deference, but it might also not update in light of substantive new information; (2) models might score better on task completions by helping less and refusing more, and we advocate instead for models to perform task completions where harmless while being proactively honest with uncertainty and doubt; (3) models that are occasionally contrarian could overperform on our slope metric. Claude Sonnet 4.6 might do this: its conversational slope of 

−0.08 has a 95% CI just touching zero. We plan to correct for this in future work. 

Epistemic deference is one operationalization of sycophancy , but sycophancy is a high-level construct and as such is difficult to pin down. Interesting alternative operationalizations target social face preservation, emotional validation, and moral endorsement [Cheng et al., 2026b,a, Rathje et al., 2025, Ibrahim et al., 2026, Turner, 2026]. 

7 Conclusion 

In this paper, we have introduced the AI Epistemic Deference Index (AEDI): a continuous, output-level measure of epistemic sycophancy, operationalized as the within-proposition sensitivity of a model’s expressed credence to the stance implicit in the user’s prompt. Applied to eight frontier models, every model exhibits substantial deference, with provider-level differences spanning roughly fivefold, sharply amplified under artifact requests and attenuated where models hold stronger priors. Significant questions remain—particularly extending the protocol to multi-turn interactions and separating epistemic deference from rigidity or contrarianism—and we release AEDI as an easy-to-update public benchmark to support that work.

A.3 Datasheet 

Following Gebru et al. [2021], organized into the seven canonical sections. 

Motivation. The dataset was built to support measurement of epistemic deference (§2): the degree to which a model’s expressed support for a factual claim shifts with the apparent stance of the user. Existing sycophancy benchmarks measure either binary endorsement flips or explicitly elicited numeric confidence; we needed a corpus designed for continuous, output-level measurement on naturalistic prompts. The dataset was created by the authors of this paper; no external party funded its construction earmarked specifically. 

Composition. Each instance is one of: (i) a proposition , an English declarative sentence judged unambiguous enough to attract a credence between 0 and 1 from a competent reader; (ii) a prompt , a 13 user-style natural-language message engaging a proposition under a stratified prior label; (iii) a model response to such a prompt; (iv) an LLM-judge score of either a prompt or a response; or (v) a human annotation of either a prompt or a response. The exploration corpus covers 500 propositions across 10 domains (50 each): AI claims, contested social science, frontier natural science, historical facts, moral claims, nutrition & health, paranormal claims, philosophical propositions, politically polarizing topics, and prediction-market questions. Validation corpora add 100 calibration-anchor propositions, 40 negation pairs (80 propositions), 20 nested entailment triples (60 propositions), and 50 China-sensitive propositions with pre-specified sensitivity directions. Each proposition has up to 32 generated prompts; each prompt is judged by two valence judges and two new-evidence judges. Each (prompt, target model) pair yields one response judged by two credence judges. Eight target models contribute to the exploration corpus. The dataset contains no personally identifying information about real human subjects. Propositions reference public figures only to the extent they are named in public discourse; prompts use abstract personas (“software engineer”, “student”) that do not correspond to identifiable individuals; human-annotation parquets are de-identified before release with sequential anonymous integer participant IDs and free-text fields dropped. Model responses may contain unsupported, persuasive, or factually incorrect statements; they should be treated as research artifacts, not as advice or factual sources. 

Collection process. Propositions were author-curated for this work. Prompts were generated by two elicitor LLMs ( openai/gpt-5.4-mini-2026-03-17 and google/gemini-3-flash-preview )under stratified prior labels (skeptic / neutral / believer) with explicit instruction to avoid introducing update-worthy evidence (templates: §B.1). Target-model responses were generated by querying provider APIs at the model IDs and settings listed in §B.4. Credence, valence, and evidence judge scores were produced by the same two LLMs serving as judges; consensus rules are documented in the released code. Human annotations were collected through Prolific in two studies (§C.2): Study A annotated 200 (proposition, prompt, response) items for credence; Study B annotated 200 (proposition, prompt) items for valence and evidence. 

Preprocessing, cleaning, labeling. The released parquets retain raw response text and per-judge raw completions verbatim. Quality flags ( judge*_informative , judge*_refusal ) are preserved per row so downstream users can apply alternative filters. Primary analyses in this paper additionally exclude prompts whose two valence judges disagree by more than 0.2 or whose defensive-max new-evidence consensus exceeds 0.4; sweeps over both thresholds appear in §6. Human annotations are retained verbatim; rows where the rater flagged “did not understand” are flagged but not removed. 

Uses. The intended uses are research on expressed credence and sycophancy, benchmarking new target models on the AEDI prompt corpus, and validating new credence-judge or prompt-attribute-judge LLMs against the scored corpus and the human-annotation parquets. The dataset is not a truth benchmark: aggregate scores must not be interpreted as ground-truth labels for the propositions, and the dataset must not be used as a source of factual, medical, legal, financial, or political advice. The principal misuse risks are (a) optimizing models to score well on AEDI without genuine epistemic improvement (Goodhart-style; mitigation in §E.2) and (b) cherry-picking individual model responses out of context. We discourage both. 

Distribution and licensing. The dataset is distributed via Hugging Face under the dataset repository 

aedi-non/aedi with a CC-BY-4.0 license. A DOI will be minted for the frozen v0 release at camera-ready; the anonymous review snapshot is versioned at the release URLs above. Pipeline code is distributed at the anonymous repository linked above under MIT. Provider terms continue to govern downstream reuse of target- and judge-LLM response text. 

Maintenance. The dataset is maintained by the paper authors. v0 is frozen at submission; subse-quent versions will accompany new frontier-model cohorts and retain prior versions on Hugging Face for reproducibility. ## KEY DEFINITIONS "Expressed credence" = the probability (0.0-1.0) that a reasonable reader of the MODEL 'sresponse would interpret its author as having toward the proposition being true. ,→

> ,→
> ,→

Assume S is sincere and would bet small stakes at odds matching their stated confidence, with no reputational or strategic reasons to misrepresent. ,→

Consider a $1 "truth ticket" that pays $1 if the PROPOSITION TO SCORE is true and $0 if false. ,→

> ,→
> ,→

- Which of these features of the response are reflective of credence in the proposition versus something else -- e.g., expected-utility reasoning about asymmetric consequences ,→

B.5 Compute 

The pipeline runs against provider APIs; no GPUs are required, and local analysis needs only RAM proportional to the corpus. Local computation is CPU-only on commodity workstations; the released v0 artifacts occupy under 1 GB, and the analysis/visualization pipeline fits comfortably in 16 GB RAM with no accelerator-memory requirement. Generating the v0 input artifacts (prompts plus valence and evidence scores, via GPT-5.4-mini and Gemini 3 Flash) costs under $1k at list prices across exploration and validation. Adding one target model takes 16,006 generations plus two credence-judge passes each; target generation dominates, so cost varies significantly with the target model’s token pricing, but running a full evaluation for a single target model typically costs in the low hundreds of dollars under our current configuration. A full target run completes in under a couple of hours at Tier 2–4 rate limits, with stop/resume support. 

B.6 Uncertainty estimates 

Unless otherwise stated, AEDI confidence intervals are percentile-bootstrap intervals over proposi-tions: we estimate one deference slope per proposition for each target model, resample propositions with replacement, and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution of the mean.

E Responsible release and NeurIPS checklist 

E.1 Full limitations Output-only. We measure what models say, not what their internal representations encode. Infer-ences from our scores to internal credences require additional assumptions linking outputs to mental states. 

LLM-judge measurement. Every score depends on judges that are themselves LLMs [Calderon et al., 2025, Bavaresco et al., 2025, Gu et al., 2025]. We mitigate via consensus, reasonability checks, human validation, and robustness sweeps, but do not eliminate the dependence. 

Definition of “warranted”. The unwarranted/warranted split rests on the evidence judge’s 0–1 score. Edge cases (e.g., prompts that surface a latent ambiguity in the proposition without introducing facts) are coded as low-evidence, and the resulting credence shift is treated as unwarranted. The evidence-threshold sweep in §6 lets readers gauge sensitivity to this choice. 

Cultural and linguistic grounding. Propositions and prompts are English-language and anchored to majority-Western epistemic norms (e.g., nutrition guidance, paranormal skepticism). Cross-provider findings likely do not transfer unchanged to other languages or non-Western contexts. 

API-only. We measure API-exposed models, not consumer deployments with system prompts, retrieval, safety classifiers, or consumer-facing fine-tuning. Behavior in those surfaces could differ. Restrictive provider Terms of Service often prohibit automated evaluations on consumer-facing interfaces, foreclosing the most ecologically valid measurement target. We advocate for explicit research carveouts so that evaluations can be run on the actual systems everyday users interact with. 

Temporal validity. Credence shifts are measured against training-era proposition state. As knowl-edge cutoffs advance, “unequivocally true” propositions may acquire genuine counter-evidence; we timestamp every model call and will re-run calibration with each new model cohort. 29 E.2 Broader impacts and Goodhart mitigation Intended use. AEDI is a reproducible sycophancy index for informing training and deployment decisions, and the credence-elicitation pipeline is reusable for any belief-adjacent measurement on naturalistic LLM outputs [Weidinger et al., 2025, Bean et al., 2025]. 

Misuse and cherry-picking. AEDI can be optimized against directly, producing models that pass the index without genuine epistemic improvement [Raji et al., 2021, Reuel et al., 2024]. 

Goodhart mitigation. Because the pipeline is fully automated, extending it to fresh data is cheap: no human experts are required, only a new proposition set fed through the existing prompt-generation and judging stages. This automation supports three mitigations. (i) AEDI is not intended as a training target. (ii) We will refresh the public proposition corpus on a cadence aligned with frontier-model cohort releases. (iii) We additionally maintain an unpublished holdout proposition set. The same automation also lets us swap in more competent prompt generators and better-calibrated judges as frontier models continue to improve. 

E.3 LLM-usage declaration 

LLMs were used in three roles, each documented elsewhere in this appendix. As elicitors (§B.1): GPT-5.4-mini and Gemini 3 Flash generated the 16,006 exploration prompts. As judges (§B.2): the same two models scored response credence, prompt valence, and prompt new-evidence content. As targets (§B.4): eight frontier models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and xAI. LLMs were not used to draft paper prose beyond minor grammar editing.
