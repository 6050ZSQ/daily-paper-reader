Abstract 

Rubric-based evaluation is a promising paradigm for judging large language model (LLM) outputs, yet self-generated rubrics lag human-annotated criteria on hard instances. We argue this discriminative gap reflects an ob-jective mismatch: self-generated rubrics de-scribe good responses, whereas effective crite-ria must discriminate between close candidates. To close this gap, we introduce SVR (S UPPORT 

VECTOR RUBRICS ), a framework that recasts rubric construction as max-margin boundary learning over preference data. SVR mines con-trastive features from preference pairs into a rubric bank, learns a prompt-conditioned se-lector together with global rubric weights, and iteratively refines the bank through support-pair selection and adversarial probing of hard neg-atives. At inference, given only the prompt, SVR retrieves the top-k rubrics from the bank and scores responses. On RubricBench, SVR narrows the gap to human reference rubrics from 24.1 to 0.3 points and outperforms strong self-rubric and judge baselines, and the learned bank transfers across judges without retrain-ing. On RewardBench 1&2, and RM-Bench, it remains competitive with dedicated reward models, demonstrating broader reward mod-eling capability. Overall, boundary-defining rubrics offer a principled route to closing the discriminative gap in LLM evaluation.

Recent evidence suggests they are not . On RubricBench (Zhang et al., 2026), a pairwise benchmark deliberately filtered for nuance and surface-level biases, replacing self-generated rubrics with human-annotated ones lifts pairwise accuracy by more than 20 percentage points with the judge model held fixed, and the gain is stable across backbones. This framing of rubrics as boundary-relevant criteria has a natural parallel in classifica-tion theory. The classical lesson from max-margin classifiers (Boser et al., 1992; Cortes and Vapnik, 1995; Bartlett et al., 1998) is that a decision bound-ary is shaped by support vectors , and by analogy an effective rubric should be tuned to the support pairs of the response space, the hard cases near the boundary, instead of to its centroid. Building on this view, we propose SVR (S UPPORT VECTOR RUBRICS ), which reframes rubric construction as max-margin boundary learn-ing over preference data. Instead of writing a fresh rubric per prompt, SVR maintains a global rubric bank contrastively mined from preference pairs, and learns a prompt-conditioned selector α(x) to-gether with a global importance weight w per rubric that define a discriminative margin between cho-sen and rejected responses. The bank is refined over rounds: samples near or on the wrong side of the margin are designated as support pairs , an ad-versarial probe synthesises hard negatives against them, and a contrastive induction step adds rubrics for the newly exposed failure modes before prun-ing low-weight, low-support, or redundant entries. At inference, SVR retrieves the top-k rubrics from the learned bank conditioned only on the prompt, fixing the evaluation criterion before any candidate response is observed. This decouples rubric gen-eration from response content, providing a princi-pled and reusable standard for downstream scoring, ranking, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) pipelines.

2 Related Work 

2.1 Rubric-based Evaluation 

Rubric-based LLM evaluation has attracted grow-ing attention, with recent benchmarks probing rubric authoring by models (Zhang et al., 2026), rubric-level human-judge agreement (Pan et al., 2026), instruction following under complex con-straints (He et al., 2025), and hierarchical rubric grading of research replication (Starace et al., 2025). On the methodological side, OpenRubrics (Liu et al., 2025b) synthesises (prompt, rubric) data via contrastive generation, RaR (Gunjal et al., 2026) and RIFL (He et al., 2025) use rubrics as RL rewards, RLCER (Sheng et al., 2026) co-trains rea-soner and rubricator, and the Implicit-to-Explicit framework of Xie et al. (2025) fixes a domain-specific core rubric set, requiring re-induction for new domains, and Rubric-ARM (Xu et al., 2026) alternately trains a rubric proposer and judge via RL for co-evolving rewards. RRD (Shen et al., 2026) mines rubrics from responses sampled by external generators, so the resulting rubrics inherit the coverage and biases of those generators. SVR departs from this landscape in several ways. Where prior methods (Liu et al., 2025b; Shen et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2025) optimise rubrics for de-scriptive coverage, summarising induced rules or sampled responses, we optimise for discriminative margin against support pairs and learn a selector that activates a subset of a dynamic global bank grown with adversarial hard negatives at the bound-ary. Unlike RRD (Shen et al., 2026), our pipeline conditions only on the prompt, and rubrics earn their place by carrying margin on misjudged sam-ples rather than by external sampling.

3 Methodology 

3.1 Problem Setup 

We start from an existing open-source preference dataset D = {(x, r +, r −)}, where r+ is preferred over r− for prompt x. Our goal is, given x, to pro-duce a small set of rubrics that lets a downstream judge score r+ above r−, with particular empha-sis on triples near the decision boundary, where self-rubric judges fail (Zhang et al., 2026). We formalize this through a pairwise margin function 

F (x, r a, r b) that aggregates prompt-conditioned, per-rubric pairwise judgments of ra against rb,and we require F (x, r +, r −) > 0. SVR com-poses F from four components: a global rubric bank as a discriminative feature space (Sec. 3.2), a sparse max-margin scoring head with a prompt-conditioned selector (Sec. 3.3), an adversarial boundary-refinement loop driven by support pairs (Sec. 3.4), and a bank pruning step (Sec. 3.5). 

3.2 Rubric Bank as Feature Space 

The criteria that separate chosen from rejected responses are, in practice, drawn from a fairly small and recurring vocabulary, factuality , com-pleteness , safety , code correctness , etc., so we ex-pect a finite rubric set to suffice across prompts (Xie et al., 2025). We therefore maintain a global bank 

B = {c1, . . . , c M } of natural-language rubrics, treated as basis dimensions of a discriminative fea-ture space shared across prompts. B is grown by 

contrastive feature induction : for each preference pair (x, r +, r −), we ask an LLM to propose rubrics that distinguish r+ from r−, then merge candidates into B via deduplication and similarity collapsing. Replacing the conventional summarisation framing 3“what makes a good response” (Liu et al., 2025b) with a contrastive framing “what rubric tells these two apart” surfaces sharper, boundary-relevant cri-teria rather than generic descriptive ones. For each rubric c, we use an LLM as a pair-wise judge that, in a single call, inspects both re-sponses side by side and returns (a) a per-response pass or fail verdict and (b) an explicit better-of-the-two preference. These signals are combined into a scalar ∆( x, r a, r b, c ) ∈ [−1.25 , 1.25] : the pass/fail difference contributes {− 1, 0, +1 } and the directional preference adds ±0.25 , so positive values favour ra and negative values favour rb, with magnitude reflecting the strength of distinction. We collect these per-rubric judgments into the pairwise differential feature 

zi(x, r a, r b) = ∆( x, r a, r b, c i), (1) so z ∈ RM encodes per rubric which response is favoured and by how much.

3.4 Adversarial Refinement 

After each fitting round, some triples in D still have margin F (x, r +, r −) ≤ τ under the current (α, w ),either misranked ( F < 0) or ranked correctly with too little confidence ( 0 ≤ F ≤ τ ). By analogy with SVM support vectors, we call these support pairs .Support-pair status is recomputed every round, so the same triple may enter and leave the support set as the boundary shifts. This gives B a bound-ary direction : it grows toward what is hard for the model right now, not what is hard on average. With-out this re-targeting, induction would accumulate generic descriptive rubrics. Static pairs saturate once the bank covers ob-vious r+/r− distinctions, so we probe the bound-ary with synthetic hard negatives that expose less-covered failure axes. For each support pair, an LLM probe receives (x, r +) and is asked to pro-duce a response that is plausible but inferior to r+

on at least one substantive criterion. The candidate set P(x) collects the original r− as a baseline, any dataset-provided alternatives, and the synthesised candidates, from which we select

4.3 Reward-Modelling Benchmarks 

Rubric-based evaluation is closely related to reward modelling, so we further evaluate on three general reward-modelling benchmarks to test broader ap-plicability beyond the rubric-focused setting. As shown in Table 2, Skywork achieves the highest average score ( 90 .3), as it is a scalar RM purpose-trained specifically for reward modelling. SVR (87.2 ) edges past the vanilla OSS baseline ( 87 .0), since holistic heuristics already account for most of the signal on these broad preference benchmarks and leave limited headroom for structured rubrics to push the score further. Indeed, both Self-Rubrics (78 .5) and Rubric-ARM ( 68 .1) fall well below OSS, as prompt-only and pipeline-generated crite-ria overwrite the holistic prior without installing a sharper substitute. SVR, by contrast, preserves that prior, further indicating that our learned rubrics are more precise than those of the baselines. At the domain level, SVR reaches 81 .9 on Pre-cise IF (RewardBench 2), outperforming Skywork (64 .1) by 17.8 points, extending the IF advantage from RubricBench. On RM-Bench, SVR stays within 1.1 points of Skywork across all three diffi-culty tiers, showing consistent competitiveness re-gardless of pair difficulty. Safety, however, remains the consistent weak spot ( 82.8 /72.4 on Reward-Bench/RewardBench 2 vs. Skywork at 95.7 /96.7 ), a domain on which Skywork has been specifi-cally trained with a dedicated preference dataset. SVR thus complements alignment-tuned RMs on instruction-heavy tasks while conceding the safety advantage to models purpose-built for it.

6 Conclusion 

SVR casts rubric construction as boundary learning over preference data. The framework jointly mines, weights, and refines a global rubric bank, and at inference retrieves only the rubrics most relevant to each prompt. On RubricBench, the resulting bank approaches human-written rubrics, and on Reward-Bench 1&2 and RM-Bench it remains competitive with dedicated reward models. Together, these re-sults suggest that the discriminative gap in current rubric-based judging is largely an artefact of how rubrics are obtained, and that boundary-defining criteria can be induced from preference signals alone without per-instance human authoring. 8Limitations 

We train the rubric bank on preference data from a single LLM judge (GPT-OSS-120B) and do not incorporate human annotations, multi-judge en-sembles, or distilled lightweight judges, so the bank inherits whatever systematic biases the train-ing judge carries. Although our method is moti-vated by max-margin classifier, the rubric bank is a discrete set of natural-language criteria without a canonical kernel feature map, so α(x) is realised as a prompt-conditioned MLP and the convergence guarantees and hyperparameter selection rules of kernelised SVR do not transfer rigorously to our setting. While the trained margin can in principle serve as a dense reward signal for reinforcement learning on open-ended generation tasks, our ex-periments only verify its discrimination quality on preference pairs and do not directly demonstrate that rubric-level gains transfer downstream to pol-icy model optimisation.

D LLM Inference Configuration 

All LLM calls in this paper share the same model and decoding configuration. We use GPT-OSS-120B served over an OpenAI-compatible chat-completions endpoint, with temperature 0.0, top-

p = 1.0, top-k disabled, and n = 1 sample per call. Whenever the model API exposes a 

reasoning_effort parameter, we keep its default value of medium for every call across every stage. The maximum generation length is 8192 tokens for pairwise rubric judging, prompt-only and con-trastive rubric induction, adversarial probe genera-tion, and rubric rewriting. The same settings are used at training and at inference, so the reported numbers reflect a single, fixed judge configuration across all baselines and SVR.

The trained bank used in our main experiments contains 1,024 rubrics with an average length of 15

H RRD Reproduction Details 

Recursive Rubric Decomposition (RRD) (Shen et al., 2026) targets LLM-as-judge and reward mod-elling through automatically mined rubrics, and is the closest prior method to SVR, so we adopt it as our main baseline. Since the authors have not released code, we reproduce the pipeline strictly from the paper and follow the RRD appendix tem-plates for all prompts. The pipeline samples exter-nal responses, proposes initial rubrics, recursively decomposes coarse ones, filters misaligned and re-dundant rubrics, assigns whitened-uniform weights, and aggregates rubric-level verdicts into a pairwise decision. RubricBench labels are used only to com-pute final accuracy, never for rubric construction or weighting. We list the key settings of our reproduction be-low. • Evaluation data. and top-p = 0 .95 .• Initial rubrics. Prompt-specific binary rubrics conditioned on the 8 samples, with a 4096 -token budget. • Recursive decomposition. A rubric is decom-posed when more than 2 of the 8 samples satisfy it, with 2 children per call, max depth 3, and a rejection threshold of 15 .• Directionality filter. Strong reference from GPT-4o and weak reference from Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), rejecting any rubric that the weak reference passes but the strong reference fails. • Redundancy filters. I Computational Cost 

We train on 35 K preference triples drawn from HelpSteer3 (Wang et al., 2025b), and a full T = 3 -round training run takes approximately 20 GPU-hours on a single 8×A100 node. The wall-clock cost of SVR is dominated by LLM judge calls. Each of the T refinement rounds issues calls in three roles, contrastive induction, adversarial prob-ing, and pairwise rubric judging, with the third accounting for the bulk of the volume. The selector and weight optimisation re-fit in minutes on CPU once the pairwise differential features z are cached, contain fewer than 1.5M trainable parameters in total, and account for under 1% of round time. At inference, SVR costs one pairwise judging call per evaluated pair, the same as the Vanilla LLM-as-a-Judge baseline. Self-Generated Rubrics requires two calls per pair, rubric generation from the prompt followed by pairwise rubric scoring. RRD pays its budget upfront, with the construction-stage call counts on RubricBench detailed in Ap-pendix H, all of which must be repeated whenever the evaluation distribution changes. SVR amor-tises a comparable budget once at training time and 18 reuses the resulting bank across downstream eval-uation sets without additional rubric-construction cost. 19
