Abstract 

We introduce a simple yet powerful framework for training large language models. In con-trast to the standard autoregressive next-token prediction based on an exact prefix, we propose a perturbation-based procedure that first transforms the prefix into a semantic neighbor and then conditions on this perturbed variant for next-token prediction. This yields a hierarchical model with a pre-post-additive noise structure. Within this framework, we develop a rigorous theory of extrapolability, namely, the capacity of a model class to make reliable predictions for token sequences that lie outside the empirical support of the training corpus. We evaluate the finite-sample performance of the proposed procedure using both synthetic and real-world language data. Results show that the proposed method consistently improves out-of-support prediction while maintaining competitive in-support performance, demonstrating that pertur-bation offers a practical route to language modeling. 

Key words and phrases: Extrapolation, large language models, perturbation, pre-post-additive noise model.

The past few years have witnessed the emergence of highly intelligent large language models (LLMs), such as the Gemini and DeepSeek series (Comanici et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). The impact of these models is everywhere: they have been deeply integrated into our daily lives and work, representing an important milestone toward the realization of artificial general intelligence. Despite their remarkable success, it has become increasingly challenging to enhance the performance of these LLMs. As the availability of high-quality text data appears to approach its limit (Villalobos et al., 2024), the scaling laws which link model performance to parameter count and data volume (Kaplan et al., 2020) suggest that performance gains from continued scaling are diminishing. This calls for more sample-efficient algorithms for further enhancement. In this paper, we propose a simple yet effective technique to bolster the performance of exist-ing LLMs, supported by a rigorous theoretical framework that characterizes its benefits. Our methodology is simple to describe. It builds upon autoregressive language models (Bengio et al., 2000) and introduces a two-step procedure for next-token prediction: (i) we generate a semantic perturbation (e.g., a synonym) of the input prefix; and (ii) the model then performs next-token prediction conditioned on this perturbed variant rather than the original input. Figure 1 provides a graphical illustration for the proposed perturbed autoregressive model. 

1.1 Motivation 

Our use of perturbations for language modeling arises naturally from research in linguistics and cognitive science, which suggests that human language comprehension and generation are funda-mentally robust to textual variation. Sophisticated cognitive processes frequently rely on analog-ical reasoning and metaphor, often remaining unaffected by semantic substitutions (Erickson and Mattson, 1981; Sato et al., 2015). As discussed by Sato et al. (2015), people habitually speak about abstract concepts metaphorically. For instance, describing a person as being “full of love” is conceptually equivalent to saying they have “a lot of love to give”. This flexibility is further supported by neurocognitive evidence. For example, Li et al. (2024) demonstrate that reasoning on syntactic patterns involves brain regions specifically dedicated to semantic analogies. Collec-tively, these findings indicate that human language production is inherently resilient to—and even driven by—textual perturbations. We bring this enlightenment to LLMs, by explicitly modeling the metaphor or analogy as a prob-abilistic perturbation process. In this paper, we adopt simple techniques (e.g., random insertion, 3Figure 1: An illustration of our approach, in comparison with the classical LLM training. In both pretrain and inference, our method processes an input prefix X<t in two steps: (i) X<t → fX<t by perturbation T (·| X<t ) and (ii) fX<t → cXt by autoregressive modeling Pθ(·| fX<t ). The classical method consists of our step (ii) only, such that X<t → cXt via autoregressive modeling Pθ(·| X<t ). deletion, and swap, see Section 2) to design the perturber, so as to pinpoint the importance of including perturbation per se. As a more important point of view, we argue that employing such a perturbed model for pretrain-ing is reasonable. Not only does it mimic the cognition of human beings, but it also addresses a crucial yet seldom studied problem in language generation—extrapolation. In our specific con-text, extrapolation can be thought of as the capability of LLMs to generate reasonable answers to unseen contexts during training. In practical applications, a model almost never encounters the exact prompts or contexts seen during training; inputs are inherently out-of-support or may even deviate substantially from the training corpus. Furthermore, we posit that the capacity for extrapolation is intrinsically linked to a model’s creativity and the emergence of complex reasoning, namely, capabilities that require the model to generalize beyond rote memorization. 4Consequently, it is expected that an LLM specifically trained to enhance its extrapolative power would outperform standard models. 

1.2 Related works 

In machine learning, extrapolation is concerned with the performance of a trained model on data points that lie outside its training support. Recent works have advanced our understanding of the extrapolability of machine learning algorithms (Christiansen et al., 2022; Dong and Ma, 2023; Kong et al., 2024). Our proposal is closely related to engression (Shen and Meinshausen, 2025), which considers the standard regression setting with a scalar response Y and a vector of predictors 

X. Its goal is to learn the conditional distribution function of Y given X. Toward that end, they distinguish among three types of regression models: (i) Post-additive noise model : Y = f (X) + ε;(ii) Pre-additive noise model : Y = f (X + η); (iii) Pre-post-additive noise model : Y = f (X + η) + ε.In all three models, f denotes a nonlinear deterministic function to be estimated, whereas ε and 

η denote some random noises. Most regression models studied in the literature fall into the first type, in which the noise is added to the response and its effect is independent of the regression function f . The second type, the pre-additive noise model, has the implication that each response 

Y is generated not directly from the corresponding predictor X, but from a perturbed version 

X + η. The third type combines the first two. Engression advocates the second type of model over the classical first type for effective distributional learning. This preference is motivated by the theoretical finding that pre-additive noise models can extrapolate under certain conditions. We will adapt the principle of engression to the large language models. Unlike Shen and Meinshausen (2025) who focus on the second type, we primarily utilize the third type of model for generality. LLMs aim to characterize the probability distribution over text. Learning such models is one of the most fundamental problems in NLP. In modern practice, these models are predominantly trained using Transformer-based autoregressive architectures (Vaswani et al., 2017; Radford et al., 2018). Pretraining refers to the procedure that encodes general linguistic knowledge into these high-capacity language models using massive human text corpora. This is typically achieved via maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), which is equivalent to minimizing the Kullback– Leibler (KL) divergence between the model’s predictive distribution and the empirical distribution 5of human texts (Radford et al., 2018). More recently, Ji et al. (2023), Zhang et al. (2023), and Ren et al. (2024) proposed to minimize alternative divergence measures, such as the total variation distance and Earth Mover’s distance, to mitigate the sensitivity of KL divergence to small perturbations in the data. Different from these works, which primarily propose alternative training objectives, we propose an alternative model architecture based on perturbation. Our model can nonetheless be trained under these objectives. 

1.3 Contribution and organization 

To summarize, we make at least the following contributions. Methodologically, we formalize a perturbed autoregressive language model, that differs from classical LLMs whose training relies exclusively on unperturbed sequences. Our approach also deviates from standard data augmen-tation that utilizes perturbations solely for enriching the training dataset. As the first of its kind, we also establish theoretical results for the proposed framework in terms of extrapolation, based on a formal characterization of the extrapolability of LLM models. Our main results establish three key properties: (i) adaptivity, demonstrating that if the base model class can extrapolate, then the class of perturbed models inherits these capabilities; (ii) robustness, spelling out some extrapolation guarantee of the perturbed models when the base model class extrapolates poorly; and (iii) extrapolability, pinpointing that with a properly chosen perturber, the perturbed models can perfectly extrapolate. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We detail the perturbed autoregressive model in Section 2, together with the choice of perturbation mechanism. Section 3 formalizes the nota-tion for extrapolation, and presents the main theoretical findings. In Section 4, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposal for pretraining through a series of experiments on both syn-thetic data and real data. Section 5 concludes. Additional experiments, configuration details of implementation, and all the proofs are included in the supplement.

Autoregressive language models are motivated by this factorization. Given a prefix X<t of length

The next token is then sampled from this distribution, and this procedure repeats for t = 1 , 2, . . . , until an end-of-sequence (EOS) token is produced, at which point the sentence is complete. Note that the stochasticity in such classical models arises solely from the sampling of the next-token distribution. Consequently, these models are conceptually similar to post-additive noise models in classical regression. Our approach differs from existing LLMs, with the intuition inspired by the favorable extrapo-lation properties of pre- and pre-post-additive noise models proposed in the standard regression setting. As discussed in Section 1, these models do not link the predictor directly to the response; instead, the response depends on a perturbed version of the predictor that resides in its neigh-borhood. Such structure inherently captures out-of-support information: even when test data lies outside the training support, the model can still extrapolate effectively provided that the test data can viewed as “neighbors” to the training data through these perturbations. We remark a technical nuance in our proposal which distinguishes our proposal from data aug-mentation. The difference lies in that we introduce perturbations not only during training (or pretraining) but also at inference time (e.g., when generating the model output), whereas data augmentation methods apply perturbations exclusively during training. That is, in our proposal, perturbation is an intrinsic component of the LLM architecture, rather than a technique to en-rich the training dataset. We will elucidate in Section 3 why such a simple change could lead to nontrivial improvement, and further demonstrate the advantages of perturbing at both training and inference time in our ablation study in Section 4.3.

We next delineate our main theoretical results. We first characterize the adaptivity of the per-turbed model class, under the following assumption. 

Assumption 1. For any Pγ , P γ′ ∈ P perturb with TV X (Pγ , P γ′ ) = 0 for all X ∈ X , their corre-sponding base models Pθ, P θ′ also satisfy TV X (Pθ, P θ′ ) = 0 for all X ∈ X .

Theorem 1 (Adaptivity) . Under Assumption 1, if Pbase is extrapolable, then Pperturb is extrap-olable. 

Assumption 1 can be viewed as an identifiability condition on the base models, which would hold if the perturbation transformation T is invertible in some sense; we refer to the supplement on how Assumption 1 can be fulfilled or relaxed. The implication of Theorem 1 is that under some identifiabili conditions on the perturber, if the base LLM can extrapolate, then the perturbed LLM will preserve this property. While this result might initially appear that nothing is gained by the perturbation itself, the following theorem demonstrates a critical safety property: even in scenarios where the base class exhibits poor extrapolability, the proposed framework remains robust to such failures. 

Theorem 2 (Robustness) . There exists a constant CT,δ depending only on T and δ, independent of Pbase , such that UPperturb (δ) ≤ CT,δ .

Theorem 2 establishes an upper bound on the extrapolation uncertainty of Pperturb that is entirely agnostic to the underlying base model class. This implies that even if the base model class Pbase 

exhibits arbitrarily large extrapolation uncertainty, the proposed perturbed model maintains a guaranteed level of robustness. This upper bound is determined solely by the properties of the perturber T and the distance δ, where the latter quantifies the deviation of the test sequence from the training support X . We give an explicit form of the constant in Theorem 2, under a concrete scenario in Proposition 1 (deferred to the end of this section). # 4 Numerical Experiments 

4.1 Synthetic data analysis 

Data generating process . In this subsection, we set the ground-truth language model P ∗ to a bi-gram language model to generate synthetic datasets for evaluation. Specifically, we set P ∗

to be such that P ∗(Xt|X<t ) = P ∗(Xt|Xt−1). As such, the synthetic data distribution is fully characterized by an initial token distribution π ∈ R|V| and a token-to-token transition matrix 

M ∈ R|V|×|V| , where |V| denotes the vocabulary size. we set π to a uniform distribution, and draw each row of the transition matrix M independently from a Dirichlet distribution with a concentration parameter of 0 .5. We sample 500 sequences from P ∗ to form a training dataset D,where each sequence has a maximum length of 10. 

Training and evaluation . To estimate P ∗, we parameterize Pθ via a neural bi-gram language model proposed by Zhang et al. (2023) and learn the model parameters via MLE. Our procedure relies on a perturbation intensity parameter that controls the variability in the perturbations. Larger values of this parameter result in more tokens in the input prefix being replaced to generate perturbed samples. In the extreme case where the parameter is set to zero, the procedure reduces to standard pretraining without perturbation. More details on Pθ and our training procedures are deferred to Appendix D.2. Since Pθ yields an estimated transition matrix cM and the oracle matrix M is known, we evaluate the model’s extrapolation capability by computing mean absolute error (MAE) between cM and M over token pairs that are absent from the training set D.

4.2 Real data analysis 

Datasets. In this subsection, we follow the experiment setup in Ren et al. (2024) and use the WikiText-2 dataset (Merity et al., 2017) for training. We evaluate the learned model’s performance on three out-of-sample datasets: (i) WikiText-103, which is more than 50 times larger than WikiText-2; (ii) the test split of the WebText dataset released by OpenAI (Radford et al., 2019); and (iii) WritingPrompts, drawn from the writing prompts forum on Reddit (Fan et al., 2018). 4.3 An ablation study 

As discussed earlier, our method employs perturbations during both training and inference. To evaluate the contribution of perturbation at each stage, we conduct an ablation study using the real data described in Section 4.2. We compare our approach against two variants: (i) TrainPer-turb , which trains the model with perturbed data but performs inference without perturbations, and is closely related to data augmentation; and (ii) TestPerturb , which trains the model on the original data while applying perturbations only during inference. Figure 3 visualizes the Mauve scores of the standard approach without perturbations during either training or inference, the two variants above, and our method, using the objective function in (2.1). It can be seen that neither TrainPerturb nor TestPerturb significantly outperforms the standard approach (i.e., without perturbation), indicating that applying perturbation at only one stage (training or inference) is insufficient to achieve meaningful performance gains. In contrast, our approach, which integrates perturbation during both training and inference, achieves substantial improvements, demonstrating the importance of perturbing at both stages. In addition, it is also interesting to see that the performance of TrainPerturb is generally unsat-isfactory (compared to NoPerturb), despite of more samples used in the training stage. However, 16 this might not be surprising—if we deem perturbation as a part of LLM architecture—as Train-Perturb discards a part of its architecture when being employed in the inference stage. 

# 5 Concluding Remarks 

We introduce a novel perturbed model class for LLM pretraining. Recent work has shown growing interest in developing statistical methodologies and foundations for LLMs, including post-training from human feedback (Lee et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024, 2025a; Lu et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Cho and Sun, 2026) or AI feedback (Xia et al., 2026), reasoning (Gong et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026a), ranking and evaluation (Fan et al., 2025a,b; Liu et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2026a), and LLM-generated text detection (Li et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b; Zhou et al., 2026b). Nonetheless, pretraining remains underexplored. Our work contributes to this growing line of literature by addressing this gap. The proposed perturbed model can be conveniently trained and deployed. Theoretically, we demonstrate that the class of perturbed models is adaptive, robust, and can extrapolate. In addition to theoretical grounding, our framework is also corroborated by the sheer empirical improvement of the pretrained LLMs. Although we choose to directly specify the perturber in this paper, it is possible to consider a parameterized perturbation class. These parameters could be jointly optimized alongside the LLM’s primary parameters to further enhance learning. Also, we expect such perturbed model class can be extended from LLMs to visual- and multimodal-LLMs. We leave the detailed development to future research.

A.1 Scoring rules as objective functions 

The parameter θ in LLMs can be computed by optimizing an objective function, for example, a proper scoring rule (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). A scoring rule S(P, v ) measures how well a token-level distribution P aligns with a specific token v. The corresponding expected score is defined as S(P, P ∗) := Ev∼P ∗ [S(P, v )]. A scoring rule is proper if S(P ∗, P ∗) ≥ S(P, P ∗) for any distributions P, P ∗. It is strictly proper if the equality holds if and only if P = P ∗. S(P, P ∗) (or minimizing the loss −S(P, P ∗)), since the optimizer exists and is unique. A prominent example is the logarithmic scoring rule, defined as S(P, v ) = log P (v). Maximizing this score over a data sequence X optimizes the log-likelihood of the data P

This theorem dives in a different route in characterizing distributional extrapolability, compared with Theorem 1 in Shen and Meinshausen (2025). It is not merely from regression to learning some categorical distributions—the real difference is essentially about the “complexity of learning”. To clarify this point, consider simple inference processes that are deterministic and injective, e.g., the setup of pre-additive noise models in Shen and Meinshausen (2025). Then the class of the overall data generating models is easily extrapolable as long as the perturbation process allows one to identify the model parameters, which would be straightforward if the pre-additive noise has full support, by applying Lemma 3. However, it would be impractical to assume that language generation admits such a simple form. We further present some theoretical results on (distributional) extrapolability which can be re-garded as a generalization of the results shown in the main text and of partial results by Shen and Meinshausen (2025), thus of independent interests. Also, recall that the total variation distance can be replaced in Definition 1. D.1 Implementation details in the training phase 

Perturbation is not applied to every token, as doing so may generate a completely random prefix. Instead, we perturb an α proportion of tokens in the prefix, where α denotes the perturbation intensity. Moreover, to be compatible with the existing training pipeline so as to simplify imple-mentation, we perturb the original dataset D to obtain a collection of new datasets eD(1) , . . . , eD(m).Finally, the combined dataset D ∪ eD(1) ∪ · · · ∪ eD(m) is used to train Pθ based on some specified scoring rule. We fix m = 1 throughout the experiments. Notably, our method makes use of joints datasets. Hence to ensure a fair comparison, we combine the original dataset and its copy when conducting the training procedure using the classical approach (i.e., without perturbation). The model parameters are then updated after each epoch.

D.3 Details on real-world language modeling 

Source of datasets. Two datasets, WikiText-2 and WikiText-103, are available from the Hug-ging Face dataset repository Salesforce/wikitext 6. As for another two datasets, Writing-Prompts and WebText, they can be accessed via the interface provided in the Github repository 

bloomberg/MixCE-acl2023 7.

Setup of methods . The MixCE method involves a tuning parameter that convexly combines the forward KL and reverse KL divergences; we fix this parameter to 0.5 in all experiments. The TVD method includes a parameter that trade-offs bias and variance, which we also set to 0.5. The MLE, EMO, and Brier methods do not involve any tuning parameters. Our method builds upon MLE, EMO, and the Brier score; for simplicity, we set the perturbation intensity α to 0.025 throughout the experiments. 

Training details . For fairness, all methods apply the same training configuration described below. We use the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5. Besides, we adopt a linear learning rate scheduler throughout training, which is trained with gradient accumulation steps set to 1. The batch size is fixed as 32. The maximum input length during training is set to 256.
